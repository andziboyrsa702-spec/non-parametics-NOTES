STA2020 Non-Parametric Statistical Techniques

A complete companion chapter for learning, calculating and interpreting the non-parametric methods in STA2020.

How to use these notes. Read the statistical idea first, then work through the ranking logic, calculation and R implementation. The aim is not to memorise commands: it is to recognise the data structure and know why a particular test is appropriate.
1

Introduction to Non-Parametric Techniques

Parametric procedures rely on a specified population model, commonly a normal model. Non-parametric procedures make weaker distributional assumptions and many of their test statistics are built from ranks, signs or frequencies rather than the original numerical magnitudes.

1.1 When are they useful?

In this course, non-parametric techniques become especially useful when quantitative observations are non-normal or their distribution is uncertain, when samples are small, or when the response is ordinal or nominal. Random sampling and the relevant independence conditions still matter.

Important. “Non-parametric” does not mean “no assumptions.” It means the procedure does not depend on a particular parametric distribution in the same way as its parametric counterpart.

1.2 Parametric versus non-parametric reasoning

Parametric techniques

Assume a known or hypothesised underlying distribution (usually normal), focus on population parameters, and typically require quantitative data. Their sampling distributions are derived under those assumptions.

Non-parametric techniques

Make weaker assumptions about the underlying distribution, do not depend on parameters specific to one distribution, and commonly use ranks, signs or frequencies. Independence and random sampling remain important.

Non-parametric methods are not restricted to non-normal data. The lecture notes emphasise that when a suitable parametric model is valid, the parametric procedure is usually preferred because it generally has greater power. Here, power is the probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis.

Nominal data consist of categories with no natural order. Ordinal categories can be ordered, although the distances between categories need not be meaningful. Interval measurements have meaningful differences but no meaningful true zero, while ratio measurements have both meaningful differences and a true zero.

Interval versus ratio — a useful distinction

Clock time has a natural order and equal differences, but there is no meaningful zero: 2 pm is not “twice” 1 pm. It is therefore interval-scaled. A duration such as seconds has a meaningful zero, so ratios are meaningful: 10 seconds really is twice 5 seconds. The lecture notes suggest applying the same reasoning to Celsius versus Kelvin.

Data-type practice from Lecture 1

Classify examples such as number of students in a class, make of car, a seven-level television rating, weekly gold closing price, month of highest sales, socioeconomic status, a five-point agreement scale, gender, maximum temperature in °C, and an excellent/good/fair/poor television rating. The point is to justify the scale from what comparisons are meaningful, not merely recognise numbers in the coding.

Core reference

Definitions You Must Understand

This reference section collects the language used throughout the lectures. Do not memorise a test name without understanding what the words in its conditions mean.

Population

The full group about which we want to make a statistical conclusion.

Sample

The observations actually collected from a population and used to make an inference.

Parametric technique

A statistical method built around assumptions about an underlying population distribution and its parameters. In this course the familiar examples typically rely on normality.

Non-parametric technique

A method that makes weaker distributional assumptions and does not depend on parameters specific to one distribution. The course procedures work mainly with ranks, signs or frequencies.

Distribution-free

A description used because many non-parametric statistics depend on relative order or signs rather than the exact numerical values and therefore do not require one specific population distribution.

Statistical power

The probability that a test rejects the null hypothesis when the alternative hypothesis is true — equivalently, avoiding a Type II error.

Nominal data

Categorical observations with no natural order, such as blood type or make of car.

Ordinal data

Categories that have a meaningful order, but numerical differences between adjacent categories are not necessarily meaningful, such as a Likert scale.

Interval data

Numerical values for which differences are meaningful but there is no true zero; ratios are therefore not meaningful. Celsius temperature is the standard example.

Ratio data

Numerical values with meaningful differences and a true zero, so ratios are meaningful. Weight is an example.

Rank

The relative position of an observation after the values have been ordered, usually from smallest to largest.

Tie

Two or more observations with the same value. They receive the average of the rank positions they occupy.

Independent samples

Samples in which membership or measurement in one sample is not paired with or dependent on membership or measurement in another.

Matched / paired samples

Two sets of measurements that are naturally linked, for example before and after measurements on the same person or deliberately matched subjects.

Location

The central position of a population distribution. In these lectures, comparisons of location are commonly described in terms of medians.

Null hypothesis (H₀)

The baseline claim tested by the procedure, such as no difference in population locations or no association.

Alternative hypothesis (H₁)

The competing claim: a difference, a directional shift, or an association. The lectures require this to be written in the context of the question.

Significance level (α)

The threshold used for deciding whether evidence against H₀ is sufficiently strong.

p-value

The probability, calculated under H₀, of obtaining a test statistic at least as incompatible with H₀ as the observed result. In the course decision rule, reject H₀ when the p-value is at most α.

Critical region

The set of test-statistic values for which H₀ is rejected. Its tail or tails depend on H₁.

Treatment

In the Friedman setting, a treatment is one of the populations/variables being compared — what is administered or compared across experimental units.

Block

A grouping of related experimental units introduced to improve treatment comparisons. Friedman ranks treatments within each block.

Association

A relationship between two variables. Association by itself must not be interpreted as cause and effect.

Monotonic relationship

A relationship where, as one variable increases, the other tends consistently to increase or consistently to decrease. Spearman targets monotonic rather than specifically linear association.

Ranking Data

Ranking is the common language of many non-parametric procedures. We replace each observation by its position after ordering the data.

2.1 Ranking without ties

Example 2.1

For the observations 4, 9, 6, 7, 5, 2, 8, order them as 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9. Their ranks are therefore 1 through 7.

2.2 Tied observations

If observations occupy the same value, assign each tied observation the average of the positions they would have occupied. If two 29s occupy positions 6 and 7, each receives rank ((6+7)/2=6.5).

Lecture 1 — multiple sets of ties

For 29, 18, 29, 19, 20, 21, 20, 33, 30, 23, 33, 33, 24, the ordered values are 18, 19, 20, 20, 21, 23, 24, 29, 29, 30, 33, 33, 33.

3

Wilcoxon Signed Rank Sum Test

This procedure compares two matched or paired samples of quantitative, non-normal data with respect to their central locations. The analysis is performed on the paired differences.

3.1 Hypotheses

The null hypothesis states that the location (median) of the paired differences is zero. The alternative may be two-sided or directional. In an assessment, state the hypotheses in the context of the question rather than only writing symbols.

3.2 Assumptions

There are two paired samples; the response is quantitative but non-normal; under the null, the population distribution of paired differences is symmetric around its median; and the paired differences are independent across pairs.

3.3 What “no effect” looks like

The lecture develops the test by imagining the same people measured before and after treatment. Define \(d_i=\text{Before}_i-\text{After}_i\). Under \(H_0\), some differences should be positive and some negative, and the sizes of gains and losses should show no systematic pattern. Ranking the absolute differences lets us detect a systematic shift without relying on normality.

The positive and negative signs are scattered across small and large ranks, so they nearly cancel. That is the behaviour expected when there is no systematic treatment shift.

3.4 Why the signed-rank idea works

Build the intuition before the formula

Imagine a treatment truly has no systematic effect. Some paired differences will be positive and some negative simply because of ordinary variation. More importantly, the large absolute differences should not all carry the same sign. Once we replace magnitudes by ranks, the positive and negative ranks should therefore roughly cancel.

If a real directional shift exists, large ranks begin accumulating on one side. That pushes the signed-rank sum (W) away from zero. This is why (Wapprox0) supports the pattern expected under (H_0), while an unusually positive or negative (W) is evidence against it.

Why remove zero differences?

A zero difference has neither a positive nor negative direction. It contributes no evidence about a shift, so it is excluded and the effective sample size (n) becomes the number of non-zero differences.

Why rank (|d_i|) instead of (d_i)?

First we measure the size of each change without allowing its sign to affect its rank. Then we reattach the sign. That separates “how large was the change?” from “which direction was the change?”

3.5 Manual R workflow — every step has a statistical meaning

From the supplied Lecture 2 R script
flexi_data$Difference <- flexi_data$Normal - flexi_data$Flexi
flexi_data <- flexi_data[flexi_data$Difference != 0, ]
n <- nrow(flexi_data)

                        ties.method = "average")
flexi_data$Signed_Rank <- flexi_data$Rank *
                          sign(flexi_data$Difference)

mean_W <- 0
sd_W <- sqrt(n*(n+1)*(2*n+1)/6)

Read that code as the test itself: difference → remove zeros → absolute size → rank → restore direction → sum. R is not doing mysterious statistics here; the code mirrors the hand calculation.

R output warning. In wilcox.test(), R reports V as the sum of the positive signed ranks. That is not always numerically identical to the signed-sum (W) used in the hand method in your lecture notes. Focus on using the correct course statistic for hand calculations and the p-value/interpretation when using R.

3.6 Exact distribution and normal approximation — see why it happens

Your supplied simulation script explicitly generates signed-rank sampling distributions under (H_0). For small (n), only a limited collection of rank-sign combinations is possible. As (n) grows, the distribution becomes increasingly smooth and bell-shaped; this is the intuition behind using a normal approximation for the large-sample procedure in the course.

Simulation idea from your supplied code
# under H0: symmetric differences around zero
diffs <- rnorm(n, mean = 0, sd = 1)
ranks <- rank(abs(diffs))
signed_ranks <- ranks * sign(diffs)
W <- sum(signed_ranks)

# theoretical spread used for the normal curve
sigma_W <- sqrt(n*(n+1)*(2*n+1)/6)

Sign Test

Coverage in your supplied notes. Lecture 1 identifies the Sign Test as the paired/matched procedure for ordinal data and gives the matched-pairs t-test as its parametric counterpart. The supplied files do not contain a full Sign Test lecture comparable to the Signed Rank, Rank Sum, Kruskal–Wallis, Friedman and Spearman lectures. This section therefore stays deliberately limited to what the source pack establishes.

The Sign Test is the simpler paired comparison in this chapter. It uses only whether a paired difference is positive or negative rather than how large that difference is. It is therefore appropriate when the paired response is ordinal, or when the magnitude/rank structure required by Signed Rank is not the focus.

4.1 Procedure

Compute paired differences, discard zero differences, count the positive and negative signs, and assess whether the observed imbalance is plausible under the null hypothesis. Under the null, positive and negative signs are equally likely.

R — exact binomial calculation
# k = number of positive non-zero differences
# n = total number of non-zero differences
binom.test(k, n, p = 0.5,
           alternative = "two.sided")

Wilcoxon Rank Sum Test

Also called the Mann–Whitney U test, this test compares the locations of two independent populations using ordinal or non-normal quantitative observations.

5.1 Data and assumptions

There are two random samples of sizes (n_1) and (n_2); samples and observations within samples are independent; and, if the populations differ, the course formulation assumes that they differ with respect to location.

5.2 Small versus large samples

The course treats the Rank Sum statistic differently according to sample size. If both sample sizes are below 10, use the exact rank-sum table. If either sample size is at least 10, use the normal approximation.

Small sample

Use \(T=T_1\). For a two-sided test, reject in either tail:

\[T\le T_L\quad\text{or}\quad T\ge T_U,\qquad T_U=n_1(n_1+n_2+1)-T_L.\]
Large sample\[z=\frac{T-\mu_T}{\sigma_T},\quad \mu_T=\frac{n_1(n_1+n_2+1)}2,\quad \sigma_T=\sqrt{\frac{n_1n_2(n_1+n_2+1)}{12}}.\]

5.3 The ranking idea

Combine the two samples, rank all (n_1+n_2) observations together, then calculate the rank sums (T_1) and (T_2). As a check,

\[T_1+T_2=\frac{(n_1+n_2)(n_1+n_2+1)}{2}.\]

If the population locations are similar, ranks should be reasonably mixed between the two samples. A systematic concentration of high or low ranks in one sample provides evidence against the null.

Visual intuition — what unequal locations look like
Sample 11.54.54.51.54.57.54.57.59T₁ = 45
Sample 2101214111416.51416.518T₂ = 126

The ranks are clearly not evenly spread: the second sample receives almost all the large ranks. This is the pattern the Rank Sum test is designed to detect.

5.4 The question that decides the test

Do not choose a test from the words “two samples”

Ask whether each observation in sample 1 has a natural partner in sample 2. If the same people are measured twice, or subjects are deliberately matched, the observations are paired and Rank Sum is not the correct procedure. If the groups contain different unrelated observational units, they are independent and Rank Sum/Mann–Whitney is the relevant two-sample rank procedure.

The STA2020 workshop makes this distinction the central recognition rule: paired → Signed Rank; independent → Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon.

6

Kruskal–Wallis Test

Kruskal–Wallis extends rank-based comparison to more than two independent groups. In STA2020 it is the non-parametric counterpart of a single-factor (one-way) ANOVA.

In plain language: Kruskal–Wallis asks whether three or more independent groups appear to be centred in the same place. It is the non-parametric counterpart of single-factor / one-way ANOVA in the supplied notes.

6.1 Conditions

The observations are ordinal or quantitative and need not be normal; treatment levels and observations within treatment levels are independent; there are at least three observations per sample; and population distributions differ only in location if they differ.

6.2 Hypotheses

(H_0): the locations of all (k) populations are the same. (H_1): at least two population locations differ.

6.3 Test statistic

Pool all observations, rank from smallest to largest, average tied ranks and obtain rank sums (T_1,ldots,T_k). With total sample size (n_T),

\[H=\frac{12}{n_T(n_T+1)}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{T_i^2}{n_i}-3(n_T+1).\]

When the null is true, ranks should be distributed fairly evenly among groups and (H) should be relatively small.

6.4 Why H becomes large

Compare two rank patterns
Uneven ranks

Sample rank sums: 6, 15, 24

H = 7.2
Evidence of separated locations
Even ranks

Sample rank sums: 14, 15, 16

H = 0.0888
What we expect when locations are similar

For the course conditions, \(H\) is compared with an upper-tail chi-squared distribution with \(k-1\) degrees of freedom. Reject \(H_0\) when \(H\) is sufficiently large or when the p-value is at most \(\alpha\).

6.5 Why Kruskal–Wallis is the natural extension

For two independent groups, Rank Sum asks whether one group tends to receive systematically larger ranks. With three or more independent groups, Kruskal–Wallis uses the same basic idea but tracks a rank sum for every group. If (H_0) is true, the groups should receive a reasonably balanced share of low, middle and high ranks. If one or more groups accumulate unusually high or low ranks, (H) increases.

The test is therefore not “ANOVA on the original observations.” The raw observations are first converted to ranks, and the between-group separation is assessed through those ranks.

6.6 EDA first, test second

Your supplied Kruskal–Wallis R file performs exploratory data analysis before calling kruskal.test(): it draws a boxplot and uses Q–Q plots for each group. This is a useful learning habit because the graph tells you what the groups look like, while the formal test tells you whether the observed rank separation is statistically convincing.

6.7 Manual calculation in R

The code reproduces the hand method
kw1$rank <- rank(kw1$num, ties.method = "average")
T1 <- sum(kw1$rank[kw1$name == "four.mid"])
T2 <- sum(kw1$rank[kw1$name == "mid.eight"])
T3 <- sum(kw1$rank[kw1$name == "eight.four"])

n <- nrow(kw1)
H <- (12/(n*(n+1))) *
     (T1^2/n1 + T2^2/n2 + T3^2/n3) -
     3*(n+1)

pchisq(H, df = 2, lower.tail = FALSE)
Why is the chi-square test upper-tailed?

Under (H_0), balanced ranks make (H) small. Evidence against (H_0) comes from unusually uneven rank sums, which make (H) large. Therefore the rejection region is in the upper tail.

Friedman Test

The Friedman test compares more than two matched or blocked samples of ordinal or non-normal quantitative data. It is the rank-based counterpart of the randomized block design analysis.

7.1 Why blocking changes the ranking

Kruskal–Wallis ranks observations after pooling independent groups. Friedman does something different: observations are ranked within each block. The rank sums are then calculated for treatments across blocks.

7.2 Assumptions

Data arise from a blocked experiment with (b) blocks. Measurements inside a block are related, measurements from different blocks are independent, and the course notes assume no block-by-treatment interaction.

7.3 Hypotheses and statistic

(H_0): all (k) treatment locations are the same. (H_1): at least two treatment locations differ.

\[F_r=\frac{12}{bk(k+1)}\sum_{j=1}^{k}T_j^2-3b(k+1).\]

When either (k) or (b) is at least 5, the course uses an approximate chi-squared distribution with (k-1) degrees of freedom.

R — two equivalent forms from the lecture code
friedman.test(y = fr.1$rating,
              groups = fr.1$manager,
              blocks = fr.1$applicant)

friedman.test(rating ~ manager | applicant,
              data = fr.1)

7.4 Class example — four managers

Applicant ratings ranked within blocks

Eight applicants form the blocks; four managers form the treatments. The lecture example obtains treatment rank sums

T₁21T₂10T₃24.5T₄24.5

With \(b=8\) and \(k=4\),

\[F_r=\frac{12}{(8)(4)(5)}[(21)^2+(10)^2+(24.5)^2+(24.5)^2]-3(8)(5)=10.61.\]

The 5% chi-squared critical value for 3 degrees of freedom is 7.815, so this statistic lies in the rejection region. In context, the example concludes that there is a difference in the way the managers evaluate candidates.

7.5 Manual R workflow

Rank inside blocks → rank sums → statistic
ranked_rating <- t(apply(wide_rating, 1, rank,
                         ties.method = "average"))
rank_sums <- colSums(ranked_rating)

n <- nrow(wide_rating) # blocks
k <- ncol(wide_rating) # treatments

Fr <- (12/(n*k*(k+1))) * sum(rank_sums^2) -
      3*n*(k+1)
p_value <- pchisq(Fr, df = k-1,
                   lower.tail = FALSE)

Spearman’s Rank Correlation Test

Spearman’s method changes the objective: rather than comparing population locations, we measure association between two variables. Both variables are at least ordinal, and the observations occur in pairs.

Association is not causation. An association may arise because variables interact, by coincidence, or because both are affected by another variable not measured in the study.

8.1 Hypotheses

(H_0:\rho_s=0): no association in the underlying population. Alternatives may state a non-zero, positive or negative association.

8.2 Calculating the coefficient

Rank (X) and (Y) separately, calculate (d_i=R(X_i)-R(Y_i)), and use

\[r_s=1-\frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}.\]

For the large-sample treatment used in the notes ((n\ge10)),

\[z=r_s\sqrt{n-1}.\]

8.4 Lecture example — study time and grade

From ranks to a test decision

The lecture example has \(n=35\) paired observations and \(\sum d_i^2=1962.5\).

\[r_s=1-\frac{6(1962.5)}{35(35^2-1)}=0.7251.\]

The large-sample statistic is

\[z=(0.7251)\sqrt{34}=4.228.\]

The notes compare this with 1.645 at the 5% level for the stated positive-direction test and report a p-value below 0.0001. The contextual conclusion is that study time and grade are positively correlated.

Choosing the Correct Test

Start with the objective and data structure, not the name of a test.

Are you comparing locations or measuring association?

Comparing locations

2 groups + paired: quantitative → Signed Rank; ordinal → Sign Test.

2 groups + independent: Rank Sum.

3+ independent: Kruskal–Wallis.

3+ matched/blocked: Friedman.

Association

Two paired ordinal/non-normal quantitative variables → Spearman rank correlation.

9.1 Recognition drills from the practice workshop

Nine matched pairs; tutoring and grades

Matched/paired quantitative observations → Wilcoxon Signed Rank. “Does tutoring affect grades?” is non-directional, so think two-sided.

Left-handed vs right-handed students

Different students in the two groups → independent → Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon. The workshop notes that with one sample already at 10, use the course normal-approximation rule.

Matched male/female executive pairs

Matched pairs → Signed Rank. “Do male executives earn more?” supplies a direction, so the alternative is one-sided.

Two separate video groups and ordinal happiness

Independent groups + ordinal response → Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon. A directional research claim determines the one-sided alternative.

9.2 A five-question decision routine

1

What is the objective? Compare locations, test randomness/frequencies, or measure association?

2

How many populations/groups? Two, three or more, or two variables measured as pairs?

3

What is the response scale? Nominal, ordinal, or quantitative?

4

Are observations independent or related? Same units twice, matched pairs and blocks create dependence.

5

What does the wording imply for (H_1)? “Different/affect” is normally two-sided; “higher/lower/positive/negative” supplies a direction.

Classic exam trap: “two samples” is not enough information. Always determine whether the samples are independent or paired/matched.
10

Advantages and Disadvantages

10.1 Advantages

The course notes emphasise that non-parametric tests remain useful when parametric methods are inapplicable or their assumptions are uncertain, can be useful for small samples, usually require relatively few assumptions, and are not restricted to quantitative data.

10.2 Disadvantages

Ranking observations or reducing them to signs discards some of the original numerical information. Consequently, when an appropriate parametric model is valid, the corresponding non-parametric procedure tends to be less efficient or less powerful. For similar sample sizes and significance levels this can mean wider confidence intervals and less frequent rejection of a false null hypothesis.

Core message

Non-parametric does not mean “better” or “worse.” Choose the procedure whose assumptions and data requirements fit the study.

Exam Workflow & Worked-Answer Structure

The tutorials and memos consistently expect more than a test name. A complete answer should show the statistical reasoning from the question to the conclusion.

1

Identify the test and justify it: objective, number of samples, data type, and independent versus paired/blocked structure.

2

State (H_0) and (H_1) in context.

3

Compute the statistic: show rankings, differences or rank sums where required.

4

Determine the rejection rule or p-value.

5

Make the decision. Compare the p-value with (alpha) (or statistic with its critical region).

6

Conclude in the language of the problem. Do not finish with only “reject (H_0).”

10.1 What your final answer should look like

Test: Name the procedure and justify it from the data structure.

Hypotheses: State both hypotheses in the language of the problem.

Calculation: Show the required ranks/differences and the statistic.

Critical region / p-value: State the rule before deciding.

Conclusion: Refer back to the original population or research question.

Final revision checklist

Can you identify the data type? Can you tell paired from independent data? Can you rank ties? Can you state contextual hypotheses? Can you calculate the statistic manually? Can you reproduce the test in R? Can you interpret the p-value in context? If yes, you understand the chapter rather than merely recognising formulas.

12.1 Property values — a complete Signed Rank example

Scenario

Ten Rondebosch properties have an assessed value and a market value. The claim is that assessed values tend to be higher. Because each property contributes both measurements, the data are paired. The direction of interest is (d= ext{Assessed}- ext{Market}>0).

Contextual hypotheses: (H_0): the median assessed-minus-market difference is zero. (H_1): the median assessed-minus-market difference is greater than zero.

Thus the sample does not provide evidence at 5% that assessed values are systematically higher.

12.2 What a strong exam answer sounds like

Do not write: “p > 0.05, accept H0.”

Write: “At the 5% significance level, we do not reject (H_0). There is insufficient evidence from this sample to conclude that assessed property values in the stated population tend to exceed market values.”

12.3 R is verification, not a replacement for understanding

The workshop places R beside the hand solution. Use that deliberately: predict the test, alternative and direction first; perform the ranking logic; then run R and explain what each argument means. If you cannot explain paired, exact and alternative, you have not yet finished learning the test.

Same function, different statistical design
# paired
wilcox.test(x, y, paired = TRUE,
            exact = FALSE,
            alternative = "greater")

# independent
wilcox.test(x, y, paired = FALSE,
            exact = FALSE,
            alternative = "two.sided")

Mastery target

You should be able to look at an unfamiliar story and explain, in words, what the observational units are, whether observations are paired or independent, what is being ranked, what (H_0) means, why the statistic becomes extreme under (H_1), which tail matters, how R represents the same design, and what the final conclusion means in the original context.
