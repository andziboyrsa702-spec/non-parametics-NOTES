Choosing the Correct Test

Start with the objective and data structure, not the name of a test.

Are you comparing locations or measuring association?

Comparing locations

2 groups + paired: quantitative → Signed Rank; ordinal → Sign Test.

2 groups + independent: Rank Sum.

3+ independent: Kruskal–Wallis.

3+ matched/blocked: Friedman.

Association

Two paired ordinal/non-normal quantitative variables → Spearman rank correlation.

9.1 Recognition drills from the practice workshop

Nine matched pairs; tutoring and grades

Matched/paired quantitative observations → Wilcoxon Signed Rank. “Does tutoring affect grades?” is non-directional, so think two-sided.

Left-handed vs right-handed students

Different students in the two groups → independent → Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon. The workshop notes that with one sample already at 10, use the course normal-approximation rule.

Matched male/female executive pairs

Matched pairs → Signed Rank. “Do male executives earn more?” supplies a direction, so the alternative is one-sided.

Two separate video groups and ordinal happiness

Independent groups + ordinal response → Mann–Whitney–Wilcoxon. A directional research claim determines the one-sided alternative.

9.2 A five-question decision routine

1

What is the objective? Compare locations, test randomness/frequencies, or measure association?

2

How many populations/groups? Two, three or more, or two variables measured as pairs?

3

What is the response scale? Nominal, ordinal, or quantitative?

4

Are observations independent or related? Same units twice, matched pairs and blocks create dependence.

5

What does the wording imply for (H_1)? “Different/affect” is normally two-sided; “higher/lower/positive/negative” supplies a direction.

Classic exam trap: “two samples” is not enough information. Always determine whether the samples are independent or paired/matched.
